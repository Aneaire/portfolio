## Q2: How do you handle multiple clients or stakeholders with conflicting priorities? Can you give an example of when you had to say "not now" and explain your rationale?

When facing conflicting priorities, my first step is to assess each request against the bigger picture. I look at the impact on other action items, whether there are dependencies, and if it aligns with the proper order of what's being implemented.

If a request doesn't fit the current priority order, I'll explain why transparently and provide a realistic timeframe for when I can address it.

For example, in a previous role, two stakeholders requested work in the same sprint. One wanted a new feature, the other needed a bug fix for an existing client. The bug fix was actively causing data issues for a paying client, while the feature had a flexible timeline. I explained to the feature stakeholder that the bug fix had to come first because it was impacting revenue and client trust, and gave them a clear date for their request. Both items got delivered without friction.

My rationale always comes down to what creates the most risk if delayed, what unblocks other work, and what aligns with the goals we've already committed to.

## Q4: Briefly describe your experience managing digital or client-facing projects. What tools have you used (e.g., Jira, Asana, Notion)?

I manage my projects primarily through **Notion**, which I chose for its flexibility as a developer-friendly platform. I use it to build dynamic dashboards that combine different views — kanban boards for task tracking, databases for client deliverables, timelines for scheduling, and embedded docs for technical specs — all in one workspace.

This lets me tailor the project hub to each project's needs rather than being locked into a rigid structure. It's especially useful for AI workflow projects where requirements evolve quickly and you need to track everything from API integrations to client feedback in one place.

Beyond Notion, I've worked with tools like **n8n** for building and managing automation workflows, **GitHub** for version control and collaboration, and **Linear** for sprint-based delivery tracking. I'm comfortable adapting to whatever tool stack a team uses — what matters most is keeping work visible, priorities clear, and stakeholders aligned.

## Q5: How do you handle unclear client requests and turn them into clear tasks or action items?

When a client request is vague, I start by asking **clarifying questions** focused on the outcome they want, not just the feature they're describing. I try to understand: What problem are they solving? Who is this for? What does "done" look like to them?

From there, I break the request into **structured tasks** with:
- A clear description of what needs to happen
- Acceptance criteria — specific, testable conditions that define completion
- Dependencies or blockers identified upfront
- An owner and a target date

If the request is still ambiguous after initial clarification, I'll draft a brief scope summary and share it back with the client for confirmation before any work begins. This prevents rework and ensures alignment early.

In practice, I've found that most unclear requests aren't truly unclear — the client just hasn't had the right questions asked yet. A 10-minute call with the right questions saves days of rework.

## Q6: How do you manage multiple projects with shifting priorities while ensuring deadlines are met?

I use a combination of **structured planning and continuous reprioritization**:

1. **Single source of truth**: I keep all projects visible in one place (Notion or Linear), with clear statuses, owners, and deadlines. No project lives only in someone's head or inbox.

2. **2-week sprint cycles**: I plan in short cycles so that shifting priorities don't derail long-term plans. Each sprint has committed items and a backlog. If priorities shift mid-sprint, I assess the trade-off explicitly — what gets deprioritized, and does the team and stakeholder agree?

3. **Daily check-ins on blockers**: I don't wait for weekly syncs to surface risks. A quick async standup or 5-minute check-in catches delays before they compound.

4. **Transparent communication**: When deadlines are at risk, I flag it early with options — not just "we're behind" but "here's what we can do: reduce scope, extend timeline, or add resources." Stakeholders appreciate clarity over surprises.

5. **Buffer for the unexpected**: I build in realistic buffers because in client-facing work, shifting priorities are not exceptions — they're the norm. Planning for 80% capacity means the 20% surprises don't break the sprint.

The key is staying organized enough to see the full picture, and communicating proactively so that shifts feel managed rather than chaotic.
